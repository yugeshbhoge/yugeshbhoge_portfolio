Distinguishing BNS and NSBH Kilonovae via Photometric Decline Rates

Establishing robust, eos and population-independent electromagnetic classification metrics to break gravitational-wave degeneracies using multi-band optical spectra.

Ish Gupta • Yugesh Bhoge • Rahul Kashyap • Mukul Bhattacharya

“Nature uses only the longest threads to weave her patterns.”

— Richard Feynman

“...Unfortunately, she also knots them so we can’t tell HMNS from LMBH.”

The Degeneracy Problem

Gravitational-wave (GW) observatories face a critical challenge: distinguishing between a high-mass ratio Binary Neutron Star (BNS) merger and a low-mass Neutron Star-Black Hole (NSBH) merger. When the primary mass falls into the rang $2.5 - 5 M_\odot$, GW signals alone yield highly degenerate parameter estimations i.e. it can't distinghuish between a HMNS (High Mass Neutron Star) and a LMBH (Low Mass Black Hole).

As wide-field optical surveys like the Rubin Observatory (LSST) come online, thousands of kilonovae will be discovered serendipitously without GW triggers. We demonstrate that the kilonova light curve itself, specifically its post-peak magnitude decline ($\Delta m$), acts as a definitive, distance-independent classifier.

Computational Pipeline

  • Population Synthesis: Simulated dense grids of $2 \times 10^4$ BNS and NSBH systems covering plausible mass, spin, and Equation of State (EoS) parameter spaces.
  • NR-Calibrated Fits: Extracted dynamical ($M_{dyn}$) and post-merger disk ($M_{disk}$) ejecta masses using numerical relativity formulae (Krüger & Foucart for NSBH; Nedora 2023 for BNS).

LSST Survey Strategy

Our findings establish a prioritization metric for the Vera C. Rubin Observatory. A rapid $\ge 3$ mag drop in the $u$-band within 48 hours is a smoking gun for an NSBH merger, allowing automated pipelines to immediately trigger expensive spectroscopic follow-up before the transient fades.

Ejecta Geometry & Composition

The observable differences in kilonovae stem from the fundamentally different ways matter is ejected during the merger, governed primarily by the electron fraction ($Y_e$) which determines lanthanide production via the r-process.

NSBH Mergers

Driven by extreme tidal disruption, NSBH mergers produce massive, highly asymmetric equatorial tidal tails. Because this dynamical ejecta comes directly from the neutron star's inner crust, it is extremely neutron-rich (low $Y_e \le 0.1$).

  • Lanthanide Fraction: High ($X_{lan} \sim 10^{-1}$)
  • Opacity ($\kappa$): Massive ($> 10 \text{ cm}^2/\text{g}$)
  • Outcome: Redder, longer-lasting emission.

BNS Mergers

BNS mergers undergo violent contact, producing shock-heated polar dynamical ejecta and massive accretion disk winds. Weak interactions (neutrino irradiation) raise the electron fraction in these regions (high $Y_e \ge 0.25$).

  • Lanthanide Fraction: Low ($X_{lan} \sim 10^{-4}$)
  • Opacity ($\kappa$): Low ($< 1 \text{ cm}^2/\text{g}$)
  • Outcome: Early, bright blue emission that fades rapidly.

Thermodynamic Framework

While our primary results rely on 3D Monte Carlo simulations, the underlying physics is beautifully captured by the Arnett-Chatzopoulos-Villar (ACV) semi-analytic formulation. The energy evolution within the expanding, radiation-dominated cloud is tracked as:

$$\frac{dE}{dt} + p\frac{dV}{dt} = \dot{Q}_{decay} - L$$

The bolometric luminosity, $L(t)$, is obtained by integrating the radioactive heating rate over time, modulated by the diffusion timescale ($t_d$). This timescale dictates how quickly photons escape the ejecta cloud:

$$L(t) = 2\,e^{-\left(\frac{t}{t_{\rm d}}\right)^2} \int_0^t \left(\frac{t'}{t_{\rm d}}\right) e^{\left(\frac{t'}{t_{\rm d}}\right)^2}\, \dot{E}(t')\,\frac{dt'}{t_{\rm d}}$$
$$t_d = \left( \frac{\kappa M_{ej}}{\beta c v_{ej}} \right)^{1/2}$$ (where geometric constant $\beta \approx 13.8$)

Use the Interactive Physics Explorer to test this equation. Higher opacity ($\kappa$) in NSBH ejecta leads to larger $t_d$, trapping heat and slowing the photometric decline.

Post-Peak Photometric Decline ($\Delta m$)

Displaying magnitude evolution in the blue u-band. The absence of high-$Y_e$ polar ejecta in NSBH events leads to a rapid collapse in blue flux ($\Delta m \sim 2.43$ at day 2). BNS events sustain this emission much longer.

Displaying magnitude evolution in the red i-band. The trend reverses: massive, high-opacity, lanthanide-rich tidal tails in NSBH mergers increase diffusion timescales, trapping heat and sustaining emission longer than BNS events.
